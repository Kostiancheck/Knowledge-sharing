# Ship of Theseus

Dota is one of my favorite examples of the [Theseus's Paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_of_Theseus), because I actually wintessed it myself as it was unfolding. I don't think there is a _single_ element in the game that remained unchanged since its first release in 2003, or even since the first version I played - 6.67c released in 2010.

## What's gone

Even if we take 2010 as a starting point, remarkably little has remained since. It is now a different game (literally), different game engine, different developer. But even if we put aside Dota 1 -> Dota 2 transition, it is really hard to find anything that hasn't changed.

Literally every hero had at least some changes. For instance, there are only 6 heroes that _still_ have the same core abilities: Lion, Shadow Shaman, Earthshaker, Lina, Vengeful Spirit and Crystal Maiden. That is, if we don't take into account Shard, Aghanim's, innate abilities and talents. 

So if Lion mainer jumped in time from 2010 to today, they would still know how stun works. Or would they? The devil is in the details. They would try to stun a hero with Linken's Sphere, and that hero wouldn't get stunned. Lion mainer would be very confused. "Why didn't it work? I casted it on the ground!!!" they would say. Yea, not a case anymore. Impale is kind of the same, but not really, because the world changed.

It's like if you wore the same jeans from 2010 today. Yes, they are still the same, they look the same, they feel the same, but they are not cool anymore. In a fashion sense, they are not the same. The world changed.

Even if we rule out 'The world changed' argument for a moment, still, literally every item, ability, creep, building in a game has changed at least a little bit. Some stuff was added, some removed, some added AND removed. Even the terrain has changed a lot.

The point is, even if we are very strict with our definition of what constitutes change, Dota has completely changed. It's a whole new ship at this point. Not a single plank remains.

## What's left

One could argue that sure concrete details might've changed, but abstract ideas and relationships are still the same. It is still a game about two teams of 5 people each coming together in an effort to destroy each other's Ancient (both the teams and the Ancient were called differently back then, but whatever). That is correct. On the hero level, Lion is still a nuker/disabler. His role in the game is mostly unchaged, and role is an abstract concept.

This is not the main point of the article, but I also find it interesting that some of the _frequencies_ hasn't changed. Runes spawn every 2 minutes, creepwaves spawn every 30 seconds, netural creeps spawn every minute. Kind of like how some [samples survive throughout the years](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jaj4B3lLh9Y), but music as a whole changes drastically.

Now, let's get back to abstract concepts. The problem that I have with abstractions here is that they are high level enough to prevent me from defying Dota.

So, abstractions let me say something like "In Dota, each team consists of 5 players". This statement has been true throughout the entirety of Dota lifetime. However, it does not help me define Dota much. I can't say "Dota is a game with two teams of 5 players each", because it is also describes CS and basketball.

That means I need to be more specific with my definition. Something like: "Dota is a game where two teams of 5 players each try to destroy enemies' Ancient", but that also covers LoL, HoN, HotS etc.

I can't even say "Dota is a game developed by Valve where two teams of 5 players each try to destroy enemies' Ancient", because it is not true. We would need to cover the fact that Dota was a map in WC3 somehow.

I can get even more specific and say "Dota is a game where two teams of 5 players each try to destroy enemies' Ancient and where you can play as Lion", since we already established that Lion is one of the most stable characters in Dota, but even this statement is not true! [HoN](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0CfqXRJ-L64) has virtually the same character.

Therefore, if I were to define dota, I would have to go insane. I would say something along the lines of "Dota is either a WC3 map or a standalone game by Valve where two teams of 5 players each try to destroy enemies' Ancient and where you can play as Lion". Yes, I have to include that last one, because [Tides of Blood](https://lanepushinggames.com/article/tides-of-blood-part-1) WC3 map fits the definition otherwise.

And it goes without saying that any definition like this would be brittle and there could very well be a version of Dota in the future that is not covered by it.

Dota can only be precisely defined externally. The very fact that I remember playing Dota 16 years ago and that I remember playing it yesterday, and that I assign both of those memories a special 'Dota slot' in my memory, is what defines Dota. I am not a solipsist, so of course we need to take into account the collective memory of all who ever played, watched or heard of Dota to make this definition more precise.

## Identity

Let's forget about Dota for a moment. What makes me _me_? How do I know that _I_ am still the same person I was 16 years ago? Or yesterday?

I believe it is common to resolve this question with your own memory. Unlike Dota, I can have memories of my own, and I do remember myself at various times of my life. And I also think this is a default mode of operation, which is why this is a common resolution. If I have never heard of Theseus's Paradox, and never asked such a question myself, I would probably just assume my own identity. My identity would be an emergent phenomenon based on my own memory. I wouldn't have identity at 1 month of age (although other people could disagree with that if I ever said this), and then suddenly I would have an identity at 3 years of age. I would also assume I had this identity uninterrupted for all this time right until now, except for that one time I blacked out.

But anyone who has read enough science fiction, or dealt with really old / really sick people knows that this resolution is quite unsatisfactory. I think people who got really drunk once know this too. "I did WHAT? How is this possible? It dosn't sound like me" they might say and then be hit with the 'proof' - a sent text message, a photo, a late night phone call recording. The more 'proofs' are presented, the less fuzzy their 'memory' becomes. I am not a neurobiologist, but I doubt the memory ever gets 'restored' in the same way as one would restore files from the Recycle bin on their computer. No, I think the 'memory' gets constructed instead.

John Perry has this really good and really short [dialogue](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil201/Perry.pdf) that puts a nail in the coffin of memory as a tool for defining identity. One of the characters there defines a clear line between 'remembering' and 'seeming to remember', and once you accept this distinction, you can't really use memory to define identity. Humans can be delusional. Our memory is not trustworthy. So why do we keep believing that it is? Why do we keep believing in our own identity?

Now we can get back to Dota. When we try to define some objects or concepts, like Dota or the Ship, we tend to do it externally. We don't assume that Dota would have its own understanding of what it is, because that would be ridiculous. Why isn't it ridiculous to think this way about ourselves then? Because our intuition tells us that we do have identity. We use "I" all the time, since we were little kids, and it feels natural to us.

Another big reason is that it is a very useful, albeit leaky, abstraction. When you cut your finger you say 'my finger hurts', you don't say 'this blob of cells is damaged, some of the cells are split open, some dead, and now these cells are separated from these cells right here'. But the reality is it was always about the cells, it was never about you.

In other terms, there is no really such a thing as identity, in the same way as there is no really such a thing as a hash map. It does not mean that we should stop using one or the other - both are very useful leaky abstractions.

## Good news

The good news is abstractions never die. There are infinitely more abstractions than concrete objects. At the same time, most of those abstractions are useless/uninteresting. But once an interesting abstraction emerges, it sticks. Even if not a single instance fits this abstraction anymore, in the eternal Universe there will be place and time where it manifests again.

## Bonus material

[John Perry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Perry_(philosopher)) is also famous for some other ideas not related to the theory of identity. You might have heard this:

> to be a high achiever, always work on something important, using it as a way to avoid doing something that's even more important

I don't know if it works, although I did find myself in similar situations before.

His another interesting idea is 'Essential indexicals'.

\<from Wikipedia>

Essential indexicals (I, here, and now) are parts of language that cannot be paraphrased away. They are seen as locating beliefs and are essential to understanding the speaker's belief. Perry presents a now-famous example to illustrate his point:

> I once followed a trail of sugar on a supermarket floor, pushing my cart down the aisle on one side of a tall counter and back the aisle on the other, seeking the shopper with the torn sack to tell him he was making a mess. With each trip around the counter, the trail became thicker. But I seemed unable to catch up. Finally it dawned on me. I was the shopper I was trying to catch.

In this example, the pronoun "I" is essentially indexical because it allowed Perry to realize that it was he himself making the mess. This realization caused him to change his behavior. Essential indexicals create the impetus for action. They cannot be paraphrased away while retaining their immediacy. If Perry were to say "Perry realized that Perry was making a mess", it would still not be essentially indexical because Perry would still have to understand that he himself is Perry. Without that extra step, there would be no reason for him to change his action. "I" is the only essential indexical in that situation.
