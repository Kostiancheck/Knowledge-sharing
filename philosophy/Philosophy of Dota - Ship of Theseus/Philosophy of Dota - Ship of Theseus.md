# Ship of Theseus

Dota is one of my favorite examples of the [Theseus's Paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_of_Theseus), because I actually witnessed it myself as it was unfolding. I don't think there is a _single_ element in the game that remained unchanged since its first release in 2003, or even since the first version I played - 6.67c released in 2010.

## What's gone

Even if we take 2010 as a starting point, remarkably little has remained since. It is now a different game (literally), different game engine, different developer. Even if we put aside Dota 1 -> Dota 2 transition, it is really hard to find anything that hasn't changed.

Literally every hero had at least some changes. For instance, there are only 6 heroes that _still_ have the same core abilities: Lion, Shadow Shaman, Earthshaker, Lina, Vengeful Spirit and Crystal Maiden. That is, if we don't take into account Shard, Aghanim's, innate abilities and talents. 

So if Lion mainer jumped in time from 2010 to today, they would still know how stun works. Or would they? The devil is in the details. They would try to stun a hero with Linken's Sphere, and that hero wouldn't get stunned. Lion mainer would be very confused. "Why didn't it work? I casted it on the ground!!!" they would say. Yea, not a case anymore. Impale is kind of the same, but not really, because the world changed.

Even if we rule out 'The world changed' argument for a moment, still, literally every item, ability, creep, building in the game has changed at least a little bit. Some stuff was added, some removed, some added AND removed. Even the terrain has changed a lot.

The point is, even if we are very strict with our definition of what constitutes change, Dota has completely changed. It's a whole new ship at this point. Not a single plank remains.

## What's left

One could argue that sure concrete details might've changed, but abstract ideas and relationships are still the same. It is still a game about two teams of 5 people coming together in an effort to destroy each other's Ancient (both the teams and the Ancient were called differently back then, but whatever). That is correct. On the hero level, Lion is still a nuker/disabler. His role in the game is mostly the same.

This is not the main point of the article, but I also find it interesting that some of the _frequencies_ hasn't changed. Runes spawn every 2 minutes, creepwaves spawn every 30 seconds, neutral creeps spawn every minute. Kind of like how some [samples survive throughout the years](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jaj4B3lLh9Y), but music as a whole changes drastically.

Now, let's get back to abstract concepts. We now determined that Dota is not _really_ about specific people developing it, or specific heroes in it, or specific items/buildings/game mechanics/design choices etc. So what _is_ Dota about then? What _is_ Dota?

That means I need to be more specific with my definition. Something like: "Dota is a game where two teams of 5 players each try to destroy enemies' Ancient", but that also covers LoL, HoN, HotS etc.

I can't even say "Dota is a game developed by Valve where two teams of 5 players each try to destroy enemies' Ancient", because it is not true. We would need to cover the fact that Dota was a map in WC3 somehow. And what if Valve goes down for some reason? I can easily imagine a future where Dota exists without Valve, because it has been the case before.

Any definition like this would be brittle and there could very well be a version of Dota in the future that is not covered by it.

So you can't really define Dota. You can only identify it. This is not unique to Dota. You would think it is easy to define chess, because it has a fairly simple and concise set of rules, but if you actually try, it will turn out to be borderline impossible. Any proper definition would have to cover both Gen Z kids playing bullet on their phones, and two old dudes playing on a physical board in the park. Yet anyone can easily identify chess when they see it.

Anything that changes _once_ already risks to lose its precise identity. And things that have changed so many times as Dota become so abstract you can't even put it into words.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BGrfhsxxmdE

Basically, the only thing that ties all of 'Dotas' together is our collective memory of its continuous evolution. Since the moment [Eul](https://dota2.fandom.com/wiki/Eul) dubbed his map as Dota, anything that people were referring to as Dota, regardless of who developed it or what has changed, was Dota.

The very fact that I remember playing Dota 16 years ago and that I remember playing it yesterday, and that I assign both of those memories a special 'Dota slot' in my memory, is basically what defines Dota.

## Identity

Let's forget about Dota for a moment. What makes me _me_? How do I know that _I_ am still the same person I was 16 years ago? Or yesterday?

We can apply the same logic here. I remember being myself 16 years ago and I remember being myself yesterday, and I assign both of those memories a special 'me slot' in my memory.

But anyone who has read enough science fiction, or dealt with really old / really sick people knows that this resolution is quite unsatisfactory. I think people who got really drunk once know this too. "I did WHAT? How is this possible? It doesn't sound like me" they might say and then be hit with the 'proof' - a sent text message, a photo, a late night phone call recording. The more 'proofs' are presented, the less fuzzy their 'memory' becomes. They seem to remember something they did, but not really. I am not a neurobiologist, but I doubt the memory ever gets 'restored' in the same way as one would restore files from the Recycle bin on their computer. No, I think the 'memory' gets constructed instead.

John Perry has this really good and really short [dialogue](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil201/Perry.pdf) that puts a nail in the coffin of memory as a tool for defining identity. One of the characters there defines a clear line between 'remembering' and 'seeming to remember', and once you accept this distinction, you can't really use memory to define identity. Humans can be delusional. Our memory is not trustworthy. So why do we keep believing that it is? Why do we keep believing in our own identity?

### Evolution

Here are some important excerpts from 'I Am a Strange Loop' by Douglas Hofstadter:

> living beings, having been shaped by evolution, have survival as their most fundamental, automatic, and built-in goal. To enhance the chances of its survival, any living being must be able to react flexibly to events that take place in its environment. This means it must develop the ability to sense and to categorize, however rudimentarily, the goings-on in its immediate environment (most earthbound beings can pretty safely ignore comets crashing on Jupiter). Once the ability to sense external goings-on has developed, however, there ensues a curious side effect that will have vital and radical consequences. This is the fact that the living being’s ability to sense certain aspects of its environment flips around and endows the being with the ability to sense certain aspects of itself.

<game about feedback loops/>

Perception and intelligence are really good for survival. Self-perception is very useful too. What about identity?

When we defined Dota, we did it externally. We didn't assume that Dota has its own understanding of what it is, because that would be ridiculous. Why isn't it ridiculous to think this way about ourselves then? 

I asked - what makes Dota _Dota_?
And then I asked - what makes me _me_?

This change looks innocent, but self-reference breaks it, just like self-reference breaks statements like 'I am lying'.

Identity is important for survival too, but not for survival of an individual, but for survival of a group. Identity accelerates communication: I, you, we, them - all of these exist only to speed up the conversation. And so are names, professions, other labels people might want to use when referencing you personally or as part of group.

This is identity. Consciousness on the other hand is not that useful for survival, if at all. I believe it is just a side effect of intelligence and self-perception.

Another big reason is that it is a very useful, albeit leaky, abstraction. When you cut your finger you say 'my finger hurts', you don't say 'this blob of cells is damaged, some of the cells are split open, some dead, and now these cells are separated from these cells right here'. But the reality is it was always about the cells, it was never about you.

In other terms, there is no really such a thing as identity, in the same way as there is no really such a thing as a hash map. It does not mean that we should stop using one or the other - both are very useful leaky abstractions.

## Good news

The good news is abstractions never die. There are infinitely more abstractions than concrete objects. At the same time, most of those abstractions are useless/uninteresting. But once an interesting abstraction emerges, it sticks. Even if not a single instance fits this abstraction anymore, in the eternal Universe there will be place and time where it manifests again.

## Bonus material

[John Perry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Perry_(philosopher)) is also famous for some other ideas not related to the theory of identity. You might have heard this:

> to be a high achiever, always work on something important, using it as a way to avoid doing something that's even more important

I don't know if it works, although I did find myself in similar situations before.

His another interesting idea is 'Essential indexicals'.

\<from Wikipedia>

Essential indexicals (I, here, and now) are parts of language that cannot be paraphrased away. They are seen as locating beliefs and are essential to understanding the speaker's belief. Perry presents a now-famous example to illustrate his point:

> I once followed a trail of sugar on a supermarket floor, pushing my cart down the aisle on one side of a tall counter and back the aisle on the other, seeking the shopper with the torn sack to tell him he was making a mess. With each trip around the counter, the trail became thicker. But I seemed unable to catch up. Finally it dawned on me. I was the shopper I was trying to catch.

In this example, the pronoun "I" is essentially indexical because it allowed Perry to realize that it was he himself making the mess. This realization caused him to change his behavior. Essential indexicals create the impetus for action. They cannot be paraphrased away while retaining their immediacy. If Perry were to say "Perry realized that Perry was making a mess", it would still not be essentially indexical because Perry would still have to understand that he himself is Perry. Without that extra step, there would be no reason for him to change his action. "I" is the only essential indexical in that situation.
