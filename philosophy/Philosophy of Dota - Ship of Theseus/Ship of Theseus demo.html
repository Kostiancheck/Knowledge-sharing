    Philosophy of Dota

    Ship of Theseus

    ~9 min read

    Dota is one of my favorite examples of Theseus's Paradox, because I actually witnessed it myself as it was unfolding. I don't think there is a single element in the game that has remained unchanged since its first release in 2003, or even since the first version I played - 6.67c released in 2010.

    What's gone

    Even if we take 2010 as a starting point, remarkably little has remained since. It is now a different game (literally), different game engine, different developer. Even if we put aside Dota 1 -> Dota 2 transition, it is really hard to find anything that hasn't changed.

    Literally every hero had at least some changes. For instance, there are only 6 heroes that still have the same core abilities: Lion, Shadow Shaman, Earthshaker, Lina, Vengeful Spirit and Crystal Maiden. That is, if we don't take into account Shard, Aghanim's, innate abilities and talents.

    So if Lion mainer jumped in time from 2010 to today, they would still know how stun works. Or would they? The devil is in the details. They would try to stun a hero with Linken's Sphere, and that hero wouldn't get stunned. Lion mainer would be very confused. "Why didn't it work? I casted it on the ground!!!" they would say. Yea, not a case anymore. Impale is kind of the same, but not really, because the world changed.

    Even if we rule out 'The world changed' argument for a moment, still, literally every item, ability, creep, building in the game has changed at least a little bit. Some stuff was added, some removed, some added AND removed. Even the terrain has changed a lot.

    The point is, even if we are very strict with our definition of what constitutes change, Dota has completely changed. It's a whole new ship at this point. Not a single plank remains.

    What's left

    One could argue that sure, concrete details might've changed, but abstract ideas and relationships are still the same. It is still a game about two teams of 5 people coming together in an effort to destroy each other's Ancient (both the teams and the Ancient were called differently back then, but whatever). That is correct. On the hero level, Lion is still a nuker/disabler. His role in the game is mostly the same.

    This is not the main point of the article, but I also find it interesting that some of the frequencies hasn't changed. Runes spawn every 2 minutes, creepwaves spawn every 30 seconds, neutral creeps spawn every minute. Kind of like how some samples survive throughout the years, but music as a whole changes drastically.

    Now, let's get back to abstract concepts. We now determined that Dota is not really about specific people developing it, or specific heroes in it, or specific items/buildings/game mechanics/design choices etc. So what is Dota about then? What is Dota?

    That means I need to be more specific with my definition. Something like: "Dota is a game where two teams of 5 players each try to destroy enemies' Ancient", but that also covers LoL, HoN, HotS etc.

    I can't even say "Dota is a game developed by Valve where two teams of 5 players each try to destroy enemies' Ancient", because it is not true. We would need to cover the fact that Dota was a map in WC3 somehow. And what if Valve goes down for some reason? I can easily imagine a future where Dota exists without Valve, because it has been the case before.

    Any definition like this would be brittle and there could very well be a version of Dota in the future that is not covered by it.

    So you can't really define Dota. You can only identify it. This is not unique to Dota. You would think it is easy to define chess, because it has a fairly simple and concise set of rules, but if you actually try, it will turn out to be borderline impossible. Any proper definition would have to cover both Gen Z kids playing bullet on their phones, and two old dudes playing on a physical board in the park. Yet anyone can easily identify chess when they see it.

    Anything that changes once already risks to lose its precise identity. And things that have changed so many times as Dota become so abstract you can't even put it into words. "You can't step into the same river twice."

    Basically, the only thing that ties all of 'Dotas' together is our collective memory of its continuous evolution. Since the moment Eul dubbed his map as Dota, anything that people were referring to as Dota, regardless of who developed it or what has changed, was Dota.

    The very fact that I remember playing Dota 16 years ago and that I remember playing it yesterday, and that I assign both of those memories a special 'Dota slot' in my memory, is basically what defines Dota.

    Identity

    Let's forget about Dota for a moment. What makes me me? How do I know that I am still the same person I was 16 years ago? Or yesterday?

    We can apply the same logic here. I remember being myself 16 years ago and I remember being myself yesterday, and I assign both of those memories a special 'me slot' in my memory.

    But anyone who has read enough science fiction, or dealt with really old / really sick people knows that this resolution is quite unsatisfactory. I think people who got really drunk once know this too. "I did WHAT? How is this possible? It doesn't sound like me" they might say and then be hit with the 'proof' - a sent text message, a photo, a late night phone call recording. The more 'proofs' are presented, the less fuzzy their 'memory' becomes. They seem to remember something they did, but not really. I am not a neurobiologist, but I doubt the memory ever gets 'restored' in the same way as one would restore files from the Recycle bin on their computer. No, I think the 'memory' gets constructed instead.

    John Perry has this really good and really short dialogue that puts a nail in the coffin of memory as a tool for defining identity. One of the characters there defines a clear line between 'remembering' and 'seeming to remember', and once you accept this distinction, you can't really use memory to define identity. Humans can be delusional. Our memory is not trustworthy. So why do we keep believing that it is? Why do we keep believing in our own identity?

    Evolution

    Here are some important excerpts from 'I Am a Strange Loop' by Douglas Hofstadter:

    living beings, having been shaped by evolution, have survival as their most fundamental, automatic, and built-in goal. To enhance the chances of its survival, any living being must be able to react flexibly to events that take place in its environment. This means it must develop the ability to sense and to categorize, however rudimentarily, the goings-on in its immediate environment (most earthbound beings can pretty safely ignore comets crashing on Jupiter). Once the ability to sense external goings-on has developed, however, there ensues a curious side effect that will have vital and radical consequences. This is the fact that the living being’s ability to sense certain aspects of its environment flips around and endows the being with the ability to sense certain aspects of itself.

    I love this quote from John Perry (again):

    I once followed a trail of sugar on a supermarket floor, pushing my cart down the aisle on one side of a tall counter and back the aisle on the other, seeking the shopper with the torn sack to tell him he was making a mess. With each trip around the counter, the trail became thicker. But I seemed unable to catch up. Finally it dawned on me. I was the shopper I was trying to catch.

    He calls words like 'I' essential indexicals, because without it he wouldn't be able to realize he was the one with ripped bag. Certain ideas are just impossible without self-reference.

    Perception and intelligence are really good for survival. Self-perception is very useful too. What about identity?

    Identity is important for survival too, but not only for survival of an individual, but for survival of a group. Identity accelerates communication: I, you, we, them - all of these exist only to speed up the conversation. And so are names, professions, other labels people might want to use when referencing you personally or as part of group.

    This is identity. It is a leaky but very useful abstraction. In other terms, there is no really such a thing as identity, in the same way as there is no really such a thing as a hash map. It does not mean that we should stop using one or the other - both are very useful leaky abstractions.

    Consciousness on the other hand is not that useful for survival, if at all. I believe it is just a side effect of intelligence and self-perception.

    So to sum up, the ability for perception is evolutionary. From that eventually comes the ability to perceive yourself. The ability to assign identities is also evolutionary. One of the identities one assigns is oneself's. From all that abilities and possible some additional unknown mechanisms, consciousness emerges.

    To sum up the sum up, identity isn't real and never has been. We are just so used to this abstraction that we confuse it for the real thing. Consciousness might or might not be real, but if it is, it definitely has 'levels' and emerges from the evolutionary need for identity.

    Good news

    It might be weird thinking of yourself as an abstraction, but I think it's a good thing.

    Abstractions never die. There are infinitely more abstractions than concrete objects. At the same time, most of those abstractions are useless/uninteresting/impossible to materialize. But once an interesting abstraction emerges, it sticks. Even if not a single instance fits this abstraction anymore, in the eternal Universe there will be time and place where it manifests again.

    Think about it as an improved version of the eternal recurrence. Once identity is established once, it will come back to reality over and over again. Some lifetimes will be exactly the same, some slightly different.

    Bonus material

    John Perry is also famous for some other ideas not related to the theory of identity. You might have heard this:

    to be a high achiever, always work on something important, using it as a way to avoid doing something that's even more important

    I don't know if it works, although I did find myself in similar situations before.

    Douglas Hofstadter is also the creator of "Hofstadter's Law", widely used in programming world although rarely named:

    Hofstadter's law: It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's law.

    Recommended reading

    Directly related:

    • A Dialogue on Personal Identity and Immortality by John Perry
    • I Am a Strange Loop by Douglas Hofstadter, chapter 6 - 'Of Selves and Symbols'

    Other:

    • Gay Science by Friedrich Nietzsche. The book is legendary in a way. It features the first appearance of Zarathustra and 'God is dead' quote. I only included it here because of chapter 341, where the concept of eternal recurrence is described
    • Gödel, Escher, Bach: An Eternal Golden Braid by Douglas Hofstadter. I would only recommend it if you really like the idea of self-reference and want to see more examples. Or, if you really want to understand Gödel's incompleteness theorem. But generally "I Am a Strange Loop" is a much nicer read.
